SCHEDULE A: Scope of Work
Project Objective: Develop a predictive model that correlates historic stream discharge patterns 
with documented channel changes in the Umatilla River. This model will establish quantifiable 
relationships between flood magnitude, stream force, and resulting geomorphic adjustments. This 
foundational work will set the stage for a subsequent phase where projected future flow regimes 
could be applied to estimate channel migration and morphological changes over the next 50-100 
years, ultimately informing effects on First Foods and guiding flood hazard mitigation strategies.
Hypothesis: Projected changes in hydrologic patterns (non-stationarity) may significantly alter the 
frequency, magnitude, and timing of geomorphic processes in the Umatilla River, leading to 
quantifiable changes in channel migration rates, floodplain connectivity, and natural feature 
structure. Specifically, increased variability between feature-restructuring large floods and 
maintenance-level moderate floods will disrupt the equilibrium of aquatic and floodplain systems 
that support First Foods.

Rationale: The 2020 Umatilla River flood demonstrated how aquatic features and freshwater 
conditions are vulnerable to hydrologic non-stationarity. The Future Flows for the Upper Umatilla 
River study forecasts increased flow variability in this watershed. As precipitation transitions 
from snow to rain, the river will likely experience higher winter flows. Regional patterns such as 
atmospheric streams are projected to increase in both frequency and intensity by the late century. 
This shift creates a concerning pattern of increased flood magnitude alongside greater variability 
between large floods that cause system disturbance with long recovery times and moderate floods 
that maintain system health.
These hydrologic shifts directly impact natural processes that sustain First Foods as described in 
the River Vision and Upland Vision reports. The Oregon Department of Geology and Mineral Industries 
(DOGAMI) has mapped historic stream channels to assess future channel migration dangers using 
Washington State's Channel Migration Zone (CMZ) protocol. CMZ mapping of the Umatilla River and 
lower McKay Creek is underway. Colorado has developed a Fluvial Danger Zone (FDZ) protocol that 
incorporates stream force (energy available for geomorphic work) associated with floods. Stream 
force scales with flood magnitude, and river channel migration is linked to large floods that 
perform more geomorphic work. Connecting stream force, flood frequency, and historic channel 
migration provides a mechanistic link between natural processes that create and maintain aquatic 
and floodplain systems.

This project seeks to understand how hydrologic non-stationarity changes the geomorphic processes 
supporting First Foods, with critical implications for natural resource management in the region. 
Understanding these natural system relationships is essential for developing effective conservation 
strategies that protect cultural resources and natural benefits under evolving land and atmospheric 
conditions. Recognizing that flood danger and channel migration are conceptually linked, we will 
develop a model correlating historic channel adjustment with the Umatilla River flood record. This 
model will function as a predictive tool to estimate potential channel adjustments under future 
flow regimes for the next 50-100 years.
